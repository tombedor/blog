# Historical precedent for the personal stance

## C. S. Lewis, “On Living in an Atomic Age” (1948)

[Primary essay, reproduced PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/philosophy/ethics/1948-lewis.pdf). Reprinted in *Present Concerns*, edited by Walter Hooper (1986), pp. 73–80. Accessed 2026-09-09.

- The opening rejects treating nuclear danger as grounds for abandoning ordinary human activities. Lewis distinguishes the existence of a lethal threat from allowing it to dominate one's mind.
- Useful short quote: “do not let us begin by exaggerating the novelty of our situation.”
- He explicitly considers the objection that destruction of civilization differs from individual death. The essay then develops a religious/metaphysical argument; it is not an empirical comparison of nuclear and earlier mortality risks.

**Connection (analysis):** close precedent for the author's clarified claim about living under involuntary catastrophic risk. It does not establish equivalent probabilities across technologies, nor that precautions or collective action are useless.

## Three distinct propositions in the author's clarification

- **Historical tractability:** where humans meaningfully direct events, familiar incentives, institutions, conflict, and defense remain relevant analytical tools.
- **Personal predicament:** ordinary people already depend on decisions made by a few actors they cannot individually control; acknowledging a new catastrophic risk need not require constant anxiety.
- **Risk magnitude:** whether AI materially changes the chance, scale, or speed of catastrophe remains an empirical question. Neither of the first two propositions answers it.

The first two are a coherent stance even if the third turns out unfavorable. Conversely, the label “human in the loop” is insufficient unless that person has information, time, and authority to intervene. Also distinguish an ordinary reader from a developer, official, or researcher with direct opportunities to reduce risk: limited individual leverage is role-dependent.
