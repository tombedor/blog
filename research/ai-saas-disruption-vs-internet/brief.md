# Research Brief: AI vs. SaaS Incumbents — Internet Disruption as Analogy

**Post:** `ai-saas-disruption-vs-internet.md`
**Date:** 2026-05-30

---

## Core Thesis to Verify

Does internet disruption of incumbents (travel agents, classifieds, encyclopedias, brokers) serve as a predictive model for how AI will disrupt SaaS? What's similar, what's structurally different?

---

## Travel Agents: The Canonical Disruption Case

**What incumbents provided:** Human intermediaries with exclusive access to airline GDS reservation systems (SABRE, Apollo), packaged expertise in routing/pricing, and relationships with suppliers. Commission-based model — 10% of ticket price, paid by airlines.

**The mechanism of disruption (two-step):**
- **Step 1 — Airlines attacked the business model:** Feb 9, 1995, Delta capped commissions at 5% (max $50 RT / $25 OW domestic); by March 2002, eight of ten largest airlines cut base commissions to zero. This removed 60% of average US travel agent revenue before the internet was even mainstream. [TravelPulse; Baltimore Sun 1997; Knowledge@Wharton]
- **Step 2 — Internet disintermediated the service:** Expedia (1996), Travelocity (1996), Priceline (1997) gave consumers direct access to the same inventory that agents once monopolized. [ESSEC Knowledge; SEC Expedia filings]

**Scale and speed:**
- Peak ARC-accredited locations: ~36,000 (1995)
- Retail agency establishments fell 59% from 1997–2013 (23,000 → under 10,000) [multiple sources confirm]
- BLS travel agent jobs 2024: ~65,700 — a fraction of the dot-com-era peak estimated at ~124,000–340,000 (conflicting sources; use range conservatively)
- Disruption window: ~7–10 years from commission cuts to structural collapse of mainstream agencies

**What happened to survivors:** Pivoted sharply upmarket — luxury/complex travel, membership fees, Virtuoso consortium alignment. The "travel agent" role still exists but is now a different product targeting a different customer.

**Key quote:** *"US airlines dramatically cut commissions in 1995 — which accounted for 60% of the average US travel agent's revenue prior to this."* [TravelPulse, "Twenty Years After the Commission Caps"]

## Classified Ads / Newspapers: The Unbundling Case

**What incumbents provided:** Bundled information product — news, reviews, and classifieds sold together. Classifieds were 40% of newspaper ad revenue and ~30% of total revenue ca. 2000.

**Mechanism:** Craigslist (1995) offered free online classifieds. Google unbundled news. Yelp unbundled reviews. Each piece was extracted and offered for free or near-free online.

**Scale and speed:**
- Classified ad revenue peaked 2000: **$19.6 billion**
- By 2012: **$4.6 billion** — a 77% drop in 12 years
- UW economist Robert Seamans (2013): Craigslist alone cost newspapers **$5 billion** in classified revenue 2000–2007
- Oxford/Review of Economic Studies peer-reviewed study: "Impact of Online Competition on Local Newspapers: Evidence from the Introduction of Craigslist" — confirms causal link

**What happened to survivors:** Many local papers folded; survivors pivoted to digital subscriptions and niche coverage. The bundled paper as a general information product mostly died.

**Canonical framing:** The "unbundling" metaphor — internet didn't improve newspapers, it disaggregated them. Each piece of value was extracted by a focused specialist offering it for free.

## Stockbrokers: The Disintermediation Case

**What incumbents provided:** Full-service brokers held exclusive knowledge of market pricing and acted as mandatory intermediaries for executing trades. Trades ran $100–$150+ each at full-service firms.

**Mechanism:** Discount brokers (Schwab 1975) started the trend; internet scaled it dramatically. E*TRADE (formed 1991), Schwab online (1995, $181.7B AUM at launch) gave consumers direct market access for $10/trade or less.

**Scale and speed:**
- By early 2000s: **18 million online trading accounts** in US; online equity trades ~25% of industry-wide volume
- Full-service firms "continually lost market share and have never fully recovered" [BLS MLR 2005; Analyzing Alpha]
- Pattern: Disintermediation, not unbundling — the intermediary was removed entirely from the transaction

**What happened to survivors:** Full-service brokers repositioned as wealth managers/advisors for high-net-worth clients. The commodity transaction layer fully moved to self-service.

## Encyclopedia Britannica: The Zero-Marginal-Cost Case

**What incumbents provided:** Authoritative, curated reference content sold as a premium physical product ($1,400–$2,000 per set). Door-to-door sales force at peak.

**Mechanism:** Microsoft Encarta (mid-1990s, CD-ROM) undercut on price first. Wikipedia (2001) made content free and continuously updated.

**Scale and speed:**
- Print sales peaked 1990: >100,000 sets per year
- Print discontinued 2012: by then, print was **1% of Britannica's revenue**
- Company survived by pivoting to online subscriptions (B2B/institutional); consumer product effectively destroyed

**Key dynamic:** When distribution cost → 0 and contribution cost → 0 (crowdsourced), the premium product's price point becomes indefensible. Not a feature battle — an economics battle.

## AI vs. SaaS Incumbents: How the Analogy Maps

**What SaaS incumbents provide:** Workflow software sold on per-seat subscription. Pricing assumes 1 human = 1 seat. Revenue scales with headcount, not output.

**The disruption mechanism:**
- AI agents can perform tasks previously requiring human workers + SaaS seats
- "A company deploys AI agents to handle tasks that previously required human workers using software tools like CRMs, support platforms, project management systems. Each worker was a seat, but as the agent absorbs the work... the seats become redundant." [MindStudio; RSM US]
- Pricing model collapses: per-seat measured access as a proxy for value; when agents replace humans, access ≠ value

**Scale signals (2025–2026):**
- Public SaaS EV/Revenue: fell from 18–19x pandemic peak to **5.1x** (March 2026)
- Estimated **>$1 trillion in market cap** wiped from sector [FinancialContent/WRAL]
- Bloomberg estimates subscription pricing declining from 60% → 30% of software pricing; outcome-based rising from 10% → 60% over next decade [Deloitte; RSM US]
- AI agent seat compression: up to 90% in some workflow categories [MindStudio]

**Key structural shift:** Pricing moving from seats → outcomes/tasks. ServiceNow example: usage-based + outcome-based pricing tied to ticket resolution times. [Bain & Company 2025]

**What's different from travel agent disruption:**
- SaaS incumbents *are* the software layer — they could embed AI and become the AI product themselves (unlike travel agents who couldn't become Expedia)
- Deterministic vs. probabilistic distinction: ERP/financial/compliance systems have regulatory moats; rules-based workflow tools (project management, simple CRM) are most exposed [Flagship Advisory Partners]
- Microsoft, SAP, Oracle precedent: survived cloud transition by embedding cloud; same playbook available for AI [Bain; Morningstar]

## Synthesis: The Structural Pattern

| Dimension | Travel Agents | Classifieds | Stockbrokers | Britannica | SaaS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Incumbent moat | Access monopoly (GDS) | Bundle lock-in | Execution monopoly | Curation/authority | Workflow integration |
| Disruption vector | Self-service + price war | Unbundling + free | Disintermediation | Zero marginal cost | Agent replaces seat |
| Speed | 7–10 years | 10–12 years | 8–10 years | 15+ years | In progress (3–5 yr?) |
| Survivor path | Luxury upmarket | Digital subscriptions | Wealth management | B2B/institutional | Embed AI or die |

**The throughline:** In every case, the incumbent's moat was **access to a scarce resource** (GDS, print distribution, exchange floor access, expensive printing). The internet made the resource non-scarce. With SaaS, the "scarce resource" was human labor + software workflows. AI agents threaten to make that non-scarce too — but the incumbents are software companies, which means they can pivot faster than a travel agency.
