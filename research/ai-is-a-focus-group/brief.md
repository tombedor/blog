# Research Brief: Focus Groups — Empirical Evidence, Notable Quotes, Product Examples

---

## EMPIRICAL TRACK RECORD

### Predictive Validity
- Focus groups are qualitative; by design they lack external validity and statistical predictive power — results are "more descriptive than predictive" (academic consensus across methodology literature)
- Revealed preference models outperform stated preference models: one study found revealed preference techniques provided **1.5x better predictions of real-world outcomes** than the best-performing stated preference technique (de Corte et al., 2021, *Health Economics*)
- Stated preferences systematically overstate willingness-to-pay and product enthusiasm — known as "hypothetical bias" in economics literature
- The "say/do gap" (also called intention-behavior gap): people consistently describe different behavior in research settings than they exhibit in the market
  - Sony yellow Walkman case: focus group loved the yellow Walkman; when participants were offered a free Walkman on exit, all chose black
  - Sony boom box case: teens in focus groups unanimously demanded color options; the colorful boom boxes sat unsold for six months

### New Product Failure Rates
- Widely cited figure of "80–90% of new products fail" is disputed; Harvard's Clayton Christensen is attributed with the 80–90% claim but reportedly disputed it
- More defensible estimates: ~25–40% of new SKUs are discontinued within 1–2 years post-launch (Springer, *Marketing Letters*, 2021)
- The inflated failure-rate figures are themselves a form of motivated reasoning by market research vendors — flagged in: Crawford (1977), *Journal of Marketing Research*, "New Product Failure Rates: Influence of Argumentum ad Populum and Self-Interest"
- No large-scale study directly compares failure rates of focus-group-informed vs. non-focus-group-informed products (gap in the literature)

### Social Desirability Bias
- Both interviews and focus groups are susceptible; focus groups may amplify it through social micro-pacts — groups collectively hide "inappropriate" attitudes (Bergen & Labonté, 2020, *Qualitative Health Research*)
- Groupthink mechanism: loudest voice dominates; quieter, dissenting views are suppressed; consensus does not represent individual opinions
- Participants "want to be nice" — reluctant to give negative feedback to a visible moderator

### The Taste Test Problem (New Coke)
- Coca-Cola conducted ~200,000 blind taste tests; New Coke beat both original Coke and Pepsi (55:45 in blind tests; 53:47 among loyal Coke drinkers)
- ~10–12% of testers expressed anger/alienation in focus group settings — researchers classified this as a small minority
- Conflict between survey data (small backlash) and focus group data (widespread emotional hostility) — Coca-Cola trusted surveys; focus groups were the more accurate signal
- Result: 400,000+ complaint calls and letters; New Coke pulled after 79 days (April–July 1985)
- Key failure: research captured *taste preference* but missed *identity/brand attachment* — the question asked was not the question that mattered

## NOTABLE QUOTES

### Steve Jobs (skeptic — verified)
- **"It's really hard to design products by focus groups. A lot of times, people don't know what they want until you show it to them."**
  - Source: *BusinessWeek*, May 25, 1998, interview by Ira Sager, Peter Burrows, Andy Reinhardt ("Steve Jobs: There's Sanity Returning")
  - Full context shows Jobs acknowledged Apple does customer research: "We have a lot of customers, and we have a lot of research into our installed base. We also watch industry trends pretty carefully. But in the end, for something this complicated, it's really hard to design products by focus groups."
  - The quote is authentic and correctly attributed; the popular truncated version loses the important nuance that Jobs was not rejecting all customer input

### Henry Ford ("faster horse" — APOCRYPHAL, do not use as verified)
- **"If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses."**
  - Status: **No verifiable primary source.** Quote Investigator found no evidence Ford said or wrote this. Not in the Henry Ford Museum's list of 200+ verified Ford quotations.
  - Earliest known link to Ford: *Cruise Industry News Quarterly*, 1999 — not a direct attribution. First direct attribution: a 2001 letter to *Marketing Week* — decades after Ford's death in 1947.
  - Ford's great-grandson William Clay Ford Jr. used it in 2006 but offered no primary evidence.
  - The concept appeared in print as early as 1930 but without Ford's name attached.
  - **Flag as apocryphal in any post that cites it.**

### Malcolm Gladwell (skeptic — from *Blink*, 2005)
- Keynoted American Association of Advertising Agencies warning of focus group drawbacks; cited Aeron chair as key case
- Argument: consumers lack the language to evaluate truly novel products; the "grammar" to describe new sensations doesn't yet exist
- Gladwell on Coke taste tests: "In the real world, no one ever drinks Coca-Cola blind" — context, branding, and packaging are bundled into the experience

### Other Skeptics
- Various ad agency practitioners and UX researchers are on record criticizing focus groups, but most quotes lack the name-recognition or verifiability to anchor a post argument
- Research industry trade press (*Quirks*, *Greenbook*) contains a "focus group is dead" discourse dating to at least 2017, with defenders pushing back

### Defenders
- Mainstream marketing academia positions focus groups as appropriate for *exploratory* research, hypothesis generation, and language development — not for prediction
- B2B International, SIS International, and other research vendors: focus groups "shine" in early-stage concept testing and uncovering the "why" behind behavior
- *PMC/NIH* literature review: focus groups valuable when the goal is understanding formulation of opinion, not frequency of opinion
- Robert Merton (inventor of the focused group interview, 1956): originally developed for wartime media analysis at the Bureau of Applied Social Research — purpose was understanding *how* people form responses, not predicting behavior

## PRODUCT CASE STUDIES

### Focus Group Input Led to Wrong Outcome
| Product | What Focus Groups Said | Actual Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **New Coke (1985)** | Preferred new taste 55:45 in blind tests | Catastrophic backlash; pulled in 79 days |
| **Sony Yellow Walkman** | Loved the yellow color | All participants chose black on exit |
| **Sony Boom Box** | Teens demanded color, not black | Colorful units sat unsold for 6 months |
| **Aeron Chair (1994)** | Comfort scores ~8/10; aesthetics never reached 6/10; described as ugly | Best-selling chair in Herman Miller history; design icon |
| **Apple "1984" ad** | Scored 5/100 on persuasion (avg. commercial: 29); deemed worst business ad ever tested | Considered one of the greatest commercials in history; widely credited with launching the Mac |

### Focus Groups Ignored, Product Succeeded
- **iPod (2001)**: Not developed from focus group input; Jobs' premise was that people "couldn't have envisioned" it — the product category didn't exist in consumer mental models
- **iPhone (2007)**: No focus group testing of the core concept reported; Jobs announced it as a product that "reinvents" three existing categories

### Post-it Notes: A More Nuanced Case
- 3M "Press 'n Peel" launched 1977 in four test cities; flopped — consumers unfamiliar with concept, some deemed it unnecessary
- Key insight came not from focus groups but from **free sample distribution** (the "Boise Blitz"): ~90% of people who *used* the product said they would buy it
- Lesson: behavioral/experiential research outperformed attitudinal research — the product needed to be experienced, not described

## WHY FOCUS GROUPS FAIL: MECHANISMS

- **Stated vs. revealed preference gap**: systematic overstatement of preferences in hypothetical settings; revealed preference models outperform by ~1.5x (de Corte 2021)
- **Hypothetical scenario bias**: people evaluate products they've never used in contexts that don't resemble real purchase decisions
- **Social desirability**: participants moderate responses toward what seems acceptable to the group and moderator
- **Groupthink**: dominant voices set direction; dissent is suppressed; output reflects social dynamics, not individual views
- **Articulation problem**: consumers lack language to evaluate genuinely novel products (Gladwell / Aeron case); unfamiliarity reads as rejection
- **Wrong question asked**: New Coke measured taste preference; the operative question was identity attachment — research can be technically rigorous and answer the wrong question
- **Context stripping**: blind taste tests, isolated product evaluations, and controlled group settings remove the brand, social, and contextual signals that drive real-world behavior

---

## ORIGINS & SCALE

- Focus group invented by sociologist Robert Merton (with Paul Lazarsfeld) in the 1940s for wartime propaganda analysis; formalized in *The Focused Interview* (Merton, Fiske, Kendall, 1956)
- Diffused into marketing research in the 1980s
- Global market research industry: >$84B revenue in 2023; in-person focus groups used by ~58% of researchers (Statista)
