# Source Note: Has Political Outreach Received Preferential Treatment?

## Provisional Verdict

**Often, but not consistently—and “preference” describes four different legal mechanisms.** The most defensible version of the hypothesis is:

> American law repeatedly privileges the circulation of political information, either by subsidizing access, excluding noncommercial advocacy from consumer-sales rules, or applying heightened constitutional protection. But privacy, election-integrity, attribution, and foreign-influence rules sometimes regulate politics as strictly as—or more strictly than—commerce.

## 1. Affirmative Subsidy or Access

These are true preferences, not merely regulatory gaps.

- **1792 newspaper postage:** Congress set newspaper rates at one cent within 100 miles and 1.5 cents beyond, while printers could exchange newspapers free. USPS says the policy promoted an informed electorate. Washington praised the post for circulating “political intelligence.”
  - https://about.usps.com/who/profile/history/periodicals-postage-history.htm
  - https://prod.millercenter.org/the-presidency/presidential-speeches/november-6-1792-fourth-annual-message-congress
- **Political-committee postal rates:** specified national/state party committees and congressional campaign committees may use Nonprofit USPS Marketing Mail prices “without regard to their nonprofit status.” Other political groups generally do not qualify, so this is a narrow institutional preference.
  - https://pe.usps.com/Archive/NHTML/DMMArchive20250407/703.htm
  - https://pe.usps.com/text/pub417/pub417_ch3_001.htm
- **Broadcast access and rates:** federal candidates receive reasonable access to broadcast facilities. Legally qualified candidates receive the station’s lowest unit charge during the 45 days before a primary and 60 days before a general election—effectively the rate offered to the most favored comparable commercial advertiser.
  - https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-307406A1.pdf
  - https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/315
  - https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/76.206

**Stated principle:** communications infrastructure should make electoral information and candidate choice available to citizens, not allocate political reach solely by wealth or gatekeeper preference.

## 2. Exclusion from Commerce-Focused Rules

This often looks like a political exemption in practice, but the statutes regulate selling rather than speech generally.

- **CAN-SPAM:** applies to messages whose primary purpose is commercial. Its legislative history says it was not intended to intrude on political, news, personal, or charitable email.
  - https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/can-spam-rule
  - https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-05-21/pdf/E8-10763.pdf
- **FTC Do Not Call / Telemarketing Sales Rule:** political solicitations are outside the rule because they do not induce the purchase of goods/services or charitable contributions. The FTC says they “are not covered by the TSR at all.”
  - https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/qa-telemarketers-sellers-about-dnc-provisions-tsr-0

**Stated principle:** these are consumer-protection rules aimed at commercial transactions, deception, and sales intrusion—not general controls on advocacy.

## 3. Heightened Constitutional Protection

- Political advocacy is at the core of the First Amendment; commercial speech receives protection, but less. Regulation that turns on a political viewpoint or topic therefore presents greater constitutional danger than a content-neutral privacy rule.
  - *Central Hudson*: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/447/557/
- In the CAN-SPAM debate summarized by Grossman, legislators offered three defenses for leaving political email out: recipients can punish annoying politicians; political speech deserves protection; and regulation could impair inexpensive campaigning by challengers.
  - https://btlj.org/data/articles2015/vol19/19_4/19-berkeley-tech-l-j-FM-1157-1575.pdf
- Grossman’s counterargument is important: inboxes are not public forums; a consent/labeling rule can be content neutral; and cheap first contact does not justify shifting unlimited sorting costs to recipients.

**Stated principle:** avoid licensing officials to decide which political speakers or messages may reach citizens, and preserve low-cost entry for insurgent or poorly financed candidates.

## 4. Institutional Self-Interest

Elected officials write the rules governing their own campaigns. Grossman notes lawmakers’ explicit worry that anti-spam legislation would hinder campaigning, and critics have long observed that incumbents benefit from easy access to constituent lists and communications channels.

Treat self-interest as an explanatory hypothesis, not a proven motive in every statute. The public-interest rationales—an informed electorate, competition, and anti-censorship—are real and appear in the record even when lawmakers also benefit.

## Counterexamples and Limits

- **TCPA cellphone rule:** political artificial/prerecorded voice calls to wireless numbers generally require prior express consent. Commercial calls face the heightened requirement of written consent, but politics is not exempt.
  - https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/64.1200
- ***Barr v. AAPC* (2020):** the Court found a government-debt exception content based, severed the exception, and left the robocall ban in place. The remedy was equal privacy protection, not a political exemption.
  - https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/19pdf/19-631_2d93.pdf
- **Attribution and election integrity:** FEC disclaimers, foreign-national spending bans, FARA labeling, voter-intimidation law, and state deepfake rules regulate political messages in ways most ordinary product advertising is not regulated.
- **Access is speaker-specific:** lowest-unit broadcast rates favor candidates, not every issue group; nonprofit postal eligibility is restricted to enumerated party committees; candidate communications still face sponsorship and recordkeeping duties.

## Interview Formulation

Avoid: “Political spam is exempt from spam law.”

Prefer: “U.S. law has repeatedly treated political reach as a public good and commercial intrusion as a consumer harm. Sometimes that creates a real subsidy; sometimes politics merely falls outside a sales statute. Privacy and election-integrity laws supply important counterexamples.”
