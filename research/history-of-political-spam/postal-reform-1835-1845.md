# Source Note: The Cheap-Postage Fight, 1835–1845

## Short Answer

The decade joined three developments that made addressed mass outreach cheaper and politically consequential: the 1835 abolitionist mail campaign exposed both the reach of mass mail and the Post Office's censorship power; Britain's 1840 Uniform Penny Post supplied a working reform model; and private U.S. carriers in 1843–1845 demonstrated that intercity letters could be carried for far less than federal rates. Congress's 1845 settlement cut and simplified postage **while strengthening the federal monopoly**. It was therefore a bargain between access and institutional survival, not a straightforward embrace of competition.

The 1835 campaign was not itself the direct cause of the 1845 act. Its importance to this project is that it showed what lower-cost, addressable political distribution could do—and why officials or recipients might resist it.

## What Happened

| Date | Development | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **1835** | The American Anti-Slavery Society mailed abolitionist papers and tracts to named Southern leaders. A Charleston mob seized and burned mail; Postmaster General Amos Kendall supported local postmasters who withheld the material. | Early mass political mail created a distribution and censorship crisis. |
| **1839–1840** | U.S. postal agent George Plitt studied British reform; Britain adopted cheap, weight-based, prepaid postage. Senator Daniel Webster introduced U.S. reduction/stamp resolutions in June 1840. | Britain supplied both a model and early revenue evidence used by each side. |
| **1840–1843** | Former New York assistant postmaster **Barnabas Bates** published, lectured, organized meetings, and circulated petitions for a low uniform rate and an end to official franking. Abolitionist editor **Joshua Leavitt** joined the campaign. | Reformers framed cheap mail as democratic and social infrastructure, not merely a merchant discount. |
| **1843–1844** | **James W. Hale** charged 6¼¢ from New York to Boston where the Post Office charged 18¾¢. **Lysander Spooner** opened the American Letter Mail Company and argued Congress could establish a post but lacked an exclusive constitutional monopoly. | Competition turned an abstract rate argument into a visible price comparison and legal challenge. |
| **1844–1845** | Postmaster General **Charles Wickliffe** resisted reductions and pursued the independents through arrests and litigation. Courts exposed gaps in the old monopoly statutes, while petitions and press agitation mounted. | The Post Office could neither ignore the cheaper services nor reliably suppress them under existing law. |
| **1845** | Congress reduced five letter-distance bands to two: **5¢ under 300 miles and 10¢ over 300 miles per half ounce**, replacing the old 6¢–25¢ per-sheet schedule. It created a cheap category for unsealed circulars and advertisements—but also expressly prohibited regular private letter expresses. | Congress bought public support with lower rates while protecting postal revenue and the national network through a stronger monopoly. |
| **1846–1847** | The Postmaster General complained that many advertisements and circulars were unclaimed or refused. Congress raised the circular rate and required prepayment in 1847 to reduce postmasters' losses. | The first special bulk-circular rate almost immediately produced a recognizable unsolicited-mail problem. |

## Who Argued for Cheaper Postage—and Why

- **Barnabas Bates** was the movement's principal organizer. A former postmaster, postal special agent, and New York assistant postmaster, he knew the institution from inside. From 1840 he used pamphlets, broadsides, public meetings, and petitions to demand a flat rate regardless of distance and abolition of the franking privilege.
- **Joshua Leavitt**, an abolitionist minister and newspaper editor, treated cheap postage as a force multiplier for reform. After observing the British system in 1843, he wrote: **“Give us the British system of postage and slavery is dead.”** His principle was direct access: cheaper letters could carry reform “from an individual to an individual,” broaden participation, and bypass hostile gatekeepers.
- **James W. Hale** made the economic case operationally. His private network carried New York–Boston letters for one-third of the federal rate and claimed 50,000–60,000 letters a day by 1845. Treat that volume as Hale's claim, not an audited figure.
- **Lysander Spooner** made the constitutional and anti-monopoly case. His 1844 pamphlet argued that the Postal Clause authorized Congress to create a postal service but did not grant it an exclusive right to carry letters.
- **Merchants and bankers** patronized and financed reform because letter postage was a business cost. **Newspaper editors, social reformers, and thousands of petitioners** supplied much of the public campaign. Senate records show rate-reduction petitions beginning in the early 1830s; many also attacked the unfairness of government officials mailing free under the franking privilege.
- **Daniel Webster** gave the issue national legislative visibility in 1840 by presenting British stamps and calling for lower rates and an inquiry into stamped prepayment, although he did not then call for ending congressional franking.

The arguments were partly compatible but not identical: affordability and family correspondence; commercial efficiency; more circulation of knowledge; equality against official franking; higher volume recapturing lost revenue; social reform; and private competition against monopoly.

## Opposition and Its Principles

- **Fiscal self-support:** Wickliffe feared a deficit. Early British reports showed postal revenue falling after the Penny Post, while the U.S. system already used expensive letter postage to subsidize free or deeply discounted newspapers and expansion across long, thinly populated routes.
- **Universal network versus cherry-picking:** private expresses served dense, profitable eastern corridors. The Post Office had national obligations. Southern and western legislators plausibly feared that revenue loss in commercial centers would threaten their newspaper and stagecoach subsidies.
- **Institutional monopoly:** Wickliffe fought Hale and Spooner with arrests, decoy letters, and litigation. The 1845 act vindicated this goal by expressly outlawing regular private letter services even as it copied their lower prices.
- **Postal livelihoods:** local postmasters were paid largely from commissions on postage collected. Rate cuts sharply reduced their compensation; unclaimed, postage-due circulars imposed handling costs without corresponding receipts.
- **Opposition to political content:** Southern mobs and postmasters resisted abolitionist mail in 1835, and Jackson sought federal suppression. This was not the main fiscal argument against cheap postage, but it demonstrated that broader access could empower disfavored speakers and provoke demands for filtering.
- **Congressional skepticism:** an early-1830s Senate committee report called reductions in letter and newspaper postage inexpedient. Senior postal administrators—including Wickliffe, Cave Johnson, Selah Hobbie, John Stuart Skinner, and, eventually, Amos Kendall—opposed or mocked cheap-postage proposals.

## Interpretation for the Political-Spam Thesis

This episode contains the later pattern in miniature: a new transport system lowers the cost of reaching selected people; activists celebrate access and incumbent gatekeepers warn about revenue, network integrity, and abuse; challengers exploit a legal definition written for older technology; lawmakers lower prices but preserve the incumbent; then unwanted bulk messages create pressure to shift costs back to senders through prepayment.

It also qualifies the “politics gets preferential treatment” hypothesis. Political information already benefited from exceptionally cheap newspaper postage, which letter writers effectively subsidized. But addressed political circulars did not receive a distinct political privilege in 1845: Congress created a content-neutral category for unsealed circulars and advertisements. Political movements benefited because their material fit the cheap format.
