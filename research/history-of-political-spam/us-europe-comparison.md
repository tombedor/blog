# Source Note: U.S. vs. Europe on Direct Political Outreach

**Status date:** October 19, 2026. **Not legal advice.** “Europe” is not one legal regime: this note separates EU-wide law, national implementation, and the United Kingdom, which is no longer an EU member.

## Bottom Line

The sharpest present-day contrast is not that Europe bans political outreach while the United States permits it.

> **The United States usually asks how the message was delivered, whether the sender is identified, and whether the communication is commercial or political. The EU also asks whether the campaign was entitled to collect, infer, combine, and use the voter data that selected the recipient.**

U.S. law therefore leaves a relatively wide path for list-based political email and SMS, especially when the equipment falls outside the TCPA's autodialer definition. EU law places campaigns inside a general data-protection regime, treats political opinions as sensitive data, and since 2025 tightly restricts personal-data targeting for online political advertising. Since August 2026, EU chatbot disclosure rules also reach the conversational interface itself.

## Current-Law Comparison

| Question | United States | European Union / European examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Privacy baseline** | No single federal GDPR-like law. Privacy remains a sectoral federal/state patchwork. Campaign outreach is governed through the TCPA, campaign-finance rules, deception law, state privacy/election laws, and private carrier rules. | GDPR applies to parties, campaigns, and vendors as data controllers/processors. Processing needs a lawful basis, transparency, purpose limitation, data minimization, and enforceable data-subject rights. ([CRS comparison](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF10896/IF10896.5.pdf); [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)) |
| **Political opinions and profiling** | Federal law does not generally classify inferred political affiliation as specially protected campaign data. Commercial voter files can combine public records, consumer data, modeled partisanship, and response history, subject to particular state/vendor rules. | Political opinions are Article 9 “special category” data. Explicit consent is one exception; narrow party/member and substantial-public-interest exceptions also exist. Inferred political opinion can count even if labeled as an “interest.” ([EDPB targeting guidance](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2021-04/edpb_guidelines_082020_on_the_targeting_of_social_media_users_en.pdf)) |

Carrier rules commonly demand opt-in, sender identity, and STOP handling. | GDPR governs when the contact data identify a person. The ePrivacy Directive requires prior consent for electronic mail used for “direct marketing,” but its application to politics is implemented/interpreted nationally. The UK expressly requires consent for political email/SMS and says the commercial “soft opt-in” does not apply. France permits consent or, in some cases, legitimate interest for political prospecting, while recommending consent and requiring an easy right to object. Manual sending does not remove GDPR duties or legitimize improperly sourced, sensitive, or profiled data. Consent may also be required under national electronic-marketing rules or the EU political-ad targeting regulation. |

## The Principles Behind the Difference

### United States: political circulation plus distrust of content controls

- Political advocacy sits at the core of the First Amendment; lawmakers are wary of rules that make officials decide which political messages may reach voters.
- Commercial spam and telemarketing statutes were built around sales transactions and consumer harm. Politics often falls outside their definitions rather than receiving a free-standing immunity.
- Campaign law tends to prefer **attribution and spending disclosure** over opt-in consent. The usual question is “who paid?” before “did the voter consent?”
- Low-cost access is defended as helping challengers and less-funded speakers reach voters, although incumbents and established campaigns also benefit.

### European Union: democratic speech within a fundamental-rights framework

- EU law treats privacy and personal-data protection as fundamental rights. Political actors do not stand outside that system merely because their purpose is democratic.
- Political opinion receives extra protection because profiling can expose or infer ideology and enable manipulation or discrimination.
- The EU does recognize a democratic interest in political organizing: GDPR Recital 56 allows member-state law to authorize some party processing of political-opinion data when the democratic system requires it, with safeguards; Article 9 has a limited political-body/member exception. This is not an absolute anti-campaign rule.
- The post-Cambridge Analytica response is visible in Regulation 2024/900: political speech may circulate, but covert sponsor identity, broker-fed microtargeting, sensitive-data profiling, and opaque targeting are treated as risks to electoral autonomy.
- Both systems restrict foreign election intervention, but through different frames: U.S. law prohibits foreign-national election spending and decision-making across channels; the EU regulation additionally tells political-ad service providers whom they may serve during a defined pre-election window.

## Direct SMS and an AI-Powered SMS: Practical Contrast

For a campaign texting a voter-file number:

1. **U.S.:** Ask whether the system uses a covered ATDS or artificial/prerecorded voice, whether consent exists if required, whether a federal/state campaign disclaimer applies, and whether state law or carrier rules impose opt-in/STOP obligations. A human click may be evidence about the system, but *Duguid* makes the equipment's number-generation capability central.
2. **EU:** Ask where the number and every targeting attribute came from, the lawful basis for each use, whether political opinion was inferred, what privacy notice and objection mechanism were supplied, whether national ePrivacy law requires consent, and—if the message is targeted online political advertising—whether the data were directly collected and separately consented to for that purpose.
3. **If an LLM replies:** In the U.S., bot identity depends on narrower federal/state doctrines. In the EU, the AI Act now generally requires the recipient to be told they are interacting with AI. Human supervision behind the system does not eliminate that interface-level duty.

The interview-ready formulation:

> In America, the loophole question is often “what machine sent this?” In Europe, it is also “what data made me the target?” With an LLM chatbot, Europe now adds a third question: “was I told the speaker was a machine?”

## Important Qualifications

- **Do not say “Europe requires consent for every political message.”** GDPR offers multiple lawful bases, the ePrivacy Directive is implemented nationally, France recognizes a possible legitimate-interest route for some political prospecting, and ordinary untargeted/in-house communications may fall outside parts of Regulation 2024/900.
- **Do not use the UK as proof of current EU law.** Its PECR/GDPR-derived rules are a useful European comparator, but it left the EU.
- **Do not say the United States has no privacy constraints.** TCPA consent rules can be strong, private lawsuits matter, some states are stricter, and carriers can block traffic that federal law might permit.
- **Do not call Regulation 2024/900 a ban on political advertising.** It is principally a transparency and targeting regime; its targeting restrictions are strongest when personal data are used for online delivery.
- **Separate legal requirement from regulator recommendation.** CNIL often recommends consent as best practice even where it describes legitimate interest as potentially available.
