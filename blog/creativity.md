Sora is dead. Is this a temporary setback on the road to AI dominance of creative fields, or is there something more fundamental at play? *Can AI be creative at all?*

I don't think so. But let's clarify terms.

## What defines creativity?

The definition of creativity is a topic of academic debate. [Runco and Jaeger](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254301596_The_Standard_Definition_of_Creativity) state that in addition to being original, a creative work must be _effective_, demonstrating *"usefulness, fit, or appropriateness."*.

In "Creativity and Culture", Stein was more explicit about the role of an audience. For Stein, to be creative a work must be "accepted as tenable or useful or satisfying by a group at some point in time."

Is an audience required? Can a work really be useful if no one sees it? The work of some great artists was discovered only after their death: One of my favorite albums, [Pink Moon by Nick Drake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_Moon), was only discovered 30 years after the songwriter's death. Was it not creative until then?

Stein's definition resonates with me. Up until after college, I was in a series of bands. While creating and playing songs with my friends was rewarding and fun, it was only when we got to perform in front of an audience that I felt real satisfaction from the creative work. So we'll go with Stein!

So with that in mind, how is AI doing?

## AI's output: A flood on top of a flood







1. What definition of creativity matters here?
    Use Stein.
    Point: creativity is not just novelty in isolation; it has to be recognized by a group.
2. AI can generate a lot of output, but output is not the same as recognized creative work.
    Put the scale numbers here: image volume, music flood, video flood.
3. Most apparent AI-content “consumption” is weak evidence of creative success.
    Distinguish:
    - passive feed exposure
    - background/utility consumption
    - outright fraudulent/bot consumption
    - chosen attention
4. The demand that does exist is uneven.
    Split by user type:
    - novelty users
    - slop/fraud operators
    - professional workflow users
    This is where Midjourney belongs as a mixed case, not a rebuttal-killer.
5. People care about authorship more than raw indistinguishability.
    Use disclosure studies and survey data here.
6. Platforms are converging on the same conclusion.
    They are not banning AI because it is fake art.
    They are regulating it as spam, fraud, deception, and low-value feed pollution.
    - meta's interesting position
7. Conclusion
    The strongest conclusion is not “AI can never emit novelty.”
    It is closer to: AI is extremely good at producing creative-looking supply, but much weaker at producing work that is socially recognized, intentionally sought out, and stably valued as creative
    work.

Thesis: Ai cannot produce anything truly novel. Supply far exceeds demand. New tools provide short lived novelty, get a burst of enthusiasm from creators, then recede into usage by AI slop creators. No demand really exist for AI generated work.
