Debate about whether the explosion of datacenter buildout will prove to be a worthwhile investment centers on two scenarios:

1. AI adoption accelerates, the datacenter investment pays out
1. AI adoption is not as fast as forecasted, and it doesn't.

This assumes that AI inference will continue to largely be handled by remote providers. But can local models eat into this market share? There are a few reasons to think so:


## Reasons the future of AI will be local

### Remote providers increase prices

The case that the unit economics of AI token providers are unsustainable has been exhaustively argued, I won't rehash it here (for the ongoing bear case, see https://www.wheresyoured.at/)

Exact figures are impossible to get, but there's good reason to believe existing remote AI subscriptions are heavily subsidized. Cursor recently estimated a $200/month Claude Max subscription [can consume up to $5,000 in compute](https://www.forbes.com/sites/annatong/2026/03/05/cursor-goes-to-war-for-ai-coding-dominance/). Granted this is coming from a rival, but the newly release [Claude Code Review](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review) lends further weight to the idea that we are in the AI version of Uber's ["decade of cheap rides"](https://slate.com/business/2022/05/uber-subsidy-lyft-cheap-rides.html)

The feature comes priced at an eye-watering [$15-$25 per PR](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/code-review#pricing), and the feature description doesn't have much to indicate why it is so superior to existing workflows as to justify a month's basic subscription cost in a single PR.

It seems as though this is a pricing experiment by Claude to see just how high a price point enterprise subscribers will tolerate.

## Open source models (continue to) catch up to frontier models relatively quickly

With the exception of gpt-4, open source models have matched performance of frontier models within 6 months of frontier model release:

<ParityChart />

Naturally, there have been accusations of open source models gaming evals, but the same could be argued of frontier models. There just does not seem to be a durable advantage in model performance.

We can expect this to continue. Startups usually try to create a moat, but model providers build have more of a waterslide: frontier models help train their open source competitors. Providers can complain about open source competitors violating terms of use in training their own models, but it's doubtfully possible to prevent.



### Small, specialized models emerge

Given today's low prices, there seems to be little downward economic pressure on token usage. People reach for the most recent model, regardless of what they are doing.

This could change as price increases, and the increasing prevalence of subagent-driven workflows provide a natural transition. I probably don't need a frontier model to fix style issues in my Python PR - a small, specialized model can fill that void. If frontier models get dramatically more (i.e. $25 per PR review) expensive, demand will increase for these models, and the open source community will be plenty able to meet it.


## Apple is betting on local

Apple is the lone contrarian amongst tech giants, in that they are not spending mountains of capital on datacenters:

https://x.com/JoshKale/status/2028889347794047071

Apple has been criticized for being "behind" on AI, but their bet appears to be: _have competitors burn cash to train models, let advances propogate into open source models, and make devices good enough to run them._

For now, running frontier open source models requires users to by specialized hradware. However, the most recent Macbook 4 pro Max looks to have made a leap in the size of model that's viable locally:

## Private and free is hard to beat

Fast, private, and free is difficult to beat. Anthropic and OpenAI have had no trouble selling Netflix-sized subscriptions, but they lack good options for building a moat.

One moat could be _memory_: if the model behaves as if it knows you and your data is held within a remote provider service, it could be a way for model providers to increase switching cost. But that comes with an _ick_ factor: not everyone wants an all knowing agent.

Local, open source tools provide a much better route for this.

Local models don't get all that much attention: there's not anyone standing to get mega-rich if they become the default option for AI. But it represents a potent threat.
